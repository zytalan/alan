# Lot in Life

A life simulator about money, built on real economic history. Inspired by
[1 in 8 Billion](https://1in8b.life/en/): you're born somewhere at random, the way
real babies are, and live one year at a time. The difference is what you decide. Here
the choices are financial — jobs, school, businesses, savings, stocks, property,
bitcoin, debt — and the end of the game tells you how much of the outcome was luck and
how much was you.

## Two ways to play

- **Be born.** Your birth year is drawn from 1950 to 2026 by how many babies the world
  had each year, or you pick it; your country is drawn weighted by how many babies each
  country had that year (World Bank births), and your family's place in the income
  distribution is random. Every year up to 2025 replays real history for your country:
  wages, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, stock and housing returns, crashes,
  bank freezes, wars. You read a year's headlines once you've lived it, as everyone did.
- **Go back.** Pick a year (1960–2015), a country and a family background, and wake up
  at 18 knowing what's coming. The game shows the headlines and market moves you
  remember. The catch is the path: you start with little money, may not have a bank
  account or access to the stock market, capital controls can block foreign assets, life
  shocks force sales at the bottom, and exchanges like Mt. Gox can take your coins.

## Decisions

An adult life meets about 60 decisions, drawn from 117 kinds, and almost all have three
or four ways to answer. Some arrive on cue: what you do after school, a first job, where
your first savings go, looking back at 30, 50 and 70. The rest come by chance. The order
is shuffled every year so no event crowds out the others. Career, family and health
events always get their turn, other chance events are held to one or two a year, and the
ones that tend to recur come back less often each time (a second lottery rollover is less
likely than the first).

A decision that comes back asks something new. The first promotion is a raise; the next
is a senior role you can take on your terms or swap for training; then your boss leaves
and a colleague you like wants the job too; then the top job, which you can share or turn
down to train the next one. Each step names the next rung of your own job's ladder, and
an executive can take part of the salary in shares that pay out if the company is ever
bought. An illness you skipped or treated on the cheap can come back worse; an injury
offers surgery or rest, a chronic disease medication for good, a grave one the choice to
spend the money on living instead. A layoff while your industry is shedding jobs says
nobody in it is hiring, and offers work outside your field, waiting it out, retraining or
freelancing. A second layoff offers a safer job or early retirement, a third a move to
where the jobs are. A second baby raises space and childcare, and a
younger child's university is measured against what the older one got. A second debt
crisis can't end in another bankruptcy within eight years, love after a marriage ends can
come with a prenup, and crashes and booms remember what you did last time.

Choices also come back as new decisions, one to nine years on, in place of that year's
chance event. The friend whose loan you co-signed stops paying. A sibling can't repay you.
The startup whose shares you hold is bought: take cash, the buyer's shares, or back the
founders again. A tenant stops paying, a patched roof fails again, or a flat bought at the
top of a boom ends up worth less than its mortgage (in the US you can hand back the keys).
A side job grows into a business. A parent you care for gets worse, a care home raises its
fees, or the siblings who did the caring can't cope. A child wants to drop out of
university. A pet falls ill. A Ponzi scheme asks you to bring in your friends. The card you
filled up is still full. Builders find rot in the flat you're renovating. An emigrant gets
homesick, a weekly commuter's family wants them home, and night classes end with an offer
in a new field.

Many choices last. A pet, car payments, a gym, cigarettes, overtime, app work, a
side job, part-time work, childcare, an employer's pension match, a lodger or money sent
home become yearly income or costs, in typical wages so they keep up with prices, until
they end. The ones tied to a job stop when the job does. Others settle later: a flat you
renovate sells two years on, and a friend may pay you back.

Some decisions belong to a place and a time: a savings circle under its local name (a
susu in Ghana, a chama in Kenya, a tanda in Mexico, an arisan in Indonesia, a hui in
China), British Gas shares in 1986, Russia's privatisation vouchers in 1992–93, the
housing booms of 2004–06 in the US, Spain, Ireland and Britain, and the 2020 lockdowns.

## Careers

At university you pick one of seven majors: computer science, engineering, business and
finance, medicine, law, education, or arts and humanities. The choice shows only what a
student could know that year: how long the degree takes, what new graduates earn, and how
fast that industry has been hiring over the last three years. Computer science is taught
from 1970 in richer countries, 1985 in middle-income ones and 1995 in the poorest, and
some majors need a minimum skill (medicine 62, law 52, computer science and engineering 45).
Vocational training picks one of nine trades the same way: nursing, electrician, IT
support, cooking and hospitality, mechanics, hairdressing, care, graphic design, or flight
school. IT is taught once IT jobs exist there, and flight school is priced in dollars
everywhere (about $45,000 a year in the US in 2020), so outside rich countries only a
well-off family can pay for it.

There are 62 jobs in 15 industries, grouped into eight areas: technology, finance and
business, engineering and industry, health and care, education and government, media and
design, shops, hotels, transport and services, and farming. Every area has work for people
without a degree, and most have work for a trade or a degree. Most people start in the work
their major or trade trains them for (software for computer science; engineering, civil
engineering or oil and gas for engineering; nursing for a nursing course), some in work it
only qualifies them for. A trade helps even where it isn't required: a trained cook is
offered more cooking jobs, and paid more, than an untrained one. Jobs come and go with
their era: web developers from 1995, cybersecurity from 2005, social media managers and
solar technicians from 2010, delivery riders from 2012, fewer typists after the 1980s and
fewer bank tellers after 2008. Salaried jobs have a five-rung ladder (junior developer,
developer, senior, lead, head of engineering) that promotions climb, and a few at the top
move on to an executive job. Years in the same industry count fully toward pay and the
rung you're hired at; years elsewhere count half, so changing field costs something.

Every job search, from your first job to the Work tab's "Look for a better job", starts
by asking where to look. Your field comes first (where you work, then what you studied),
then the other areas, then "Anything going". Each shows the work you'd most likely be
offered, what it pays you and how its hiring went last year; an area with nothing open to
you says why ("Needs nursing training or a degree in medicine"). Then come the openings
there: four where you trained or worked and three elsewhere in ordinary times, fewer
when the area is shedding jobs, and none when it is collapsing ("Nobody in technology is
hiring right now"). You can take one, look somewhere else (an area with nothing stays shut for the
year, and the openings don't change if you look again) or keep looking for a year.

Every industry in every country has a real yearly change in its number of jobs: the US
Bureau of Labor Statistics' employment by industry since 1940, and the ILO's estimates for
every country since 1991. Before 1991, industries that move worldwide (tech, finance, oil,
factories) follow the US and the rest follow the country's own economy, plus shocks the
economy doesn't show, such as Britain's pit closures and Latin America's debt crisis. After
2025 they are simulated. That number decides how many offers come, how fast pay rises,
who is laid off (the newest hires first) and which decisions arrive: a bidding war for you
in a boom; a hiring freeze, pay cut or buyout in a bust; and a dying trade when your job is
fading.

Checked against history: US software developers hired in 1999 were laid off in 2001–03
about twice as often (23%) as those hired in 1993 over 1995–97 (11%); Spanish civil
engineers 60% in 2008–12 against 17% in 2002–06; US nurses 4% through 2008–10. A new
computer science graduate looking in technology finds four openings in 1998 and two in
2002; a Spanish engineering graduate finds four in engineering and industry in 2007 and
none in 2009. At 40, a US graduate born in 1975 earns, in typical wages: medicine 5.1,
computer science 4.5, law 3.9, engineering 3.2, business 3.2, education 1.9, arts 1.9.

## The trading desk

From 18, with a bank account and a stock market to reach, you can open a brokerage
account and play any year month by month. Move money in from savings, buy and sell
your country's index fund, the S&P 500 (where capital controls allow) and listed
companies, set limit and stop-loss orders, and press "run until something happens" to
skip to the next fill, 10% move (30% for coins) or major headline. Orders fill at the month's average
price plus a spread and the commission of the era (1.5% before 1975, 0.05% today);
index funds charge the fees of their day and pay dividends. There is no borrowing and
no short selling. Leave the desk and the rest of the year plays out on its own; gains
are taxed at year end and the account counts in your net worth.

Coins trade there too: Bitcoin from August 2010, then Litecoin, Ether, XRP, Dogecoin,
Solana and LUNA as each reached a big exchange, at their real monthly averages. Early
coin markets were thin, so spreads start at four times today's and the exchange fee at
Mt. Gox's 0.6%. Coins stay on the exchange unless you move them to your own wallet for
a 0.2% network fee. An exchange can fail with them inside: Mt. Gox in 2014 (a fifth of
the coins came back in 2024), QuadrigaCX in Canada in 2019, Thodex in Turkey in 2021
and FTX in 2022 (repaid in cash in 2025, at 119% of their November 2022 value). A
wallet survives all of that, but about one in a hundred loses its keys each year. Where
banks wouldn't serve exchanges (China 2017–2021, India 2018–2020, Nigeria 2021–2023)
coins change hands person to person, about 2% worse each way. Where they were banned
outright (China since September 2021, Bolivia 2014–2024, Bangladesh, Nepal, Morocco,
Iraq, Algeria, Qatar, Egypt, Afghanistan) you can only sell what you already hold, and
your savings plan can't buy them either. The coins in your savings plan use the same
exchange, so a failure takes what you left on it in both places, and repays both.
After the data ends, Bitcoin's months end each year at the price your savings use; the
other coins follow Bitcoin with their own swings and a small yearly chance of
collapsing, as LUNA did in May 2022.

Gold and silver trade there too, at the World Bank's monthly prices since 1960: bars and
coins from a dealer, with a 2% spread (5% for silver) and vault fees, until funds made
them cheap (2005 for gold, 2007 for silver). Owning gold follows the law of the place:
Americans may not hold gold bars or coins until the last day of 1974, Indians only
jewellery from 1963 until the Gold (Control) Act is repealed in June 1990, and in
Britain gold bars stay off limits until exchange controls end in 1979, so gold comes as
coins at a premium (and not at all from April 1966 to March 1971). The same rules apply
to the savings plan. After 2025 gold ends each year at the life engine's gold return;
silver follows gold through a gold-to-silver ratio that drifts back towards 70.

Companies are fictional, but each belongs to a real US industry and moves with it
month by month (Kenneth French's 49 industry portfolios since 1926), on top of your
country's index and its own luck: listings, takeovers, breakout years and
bankruptcies, which come more often in crashes and, for banks, in banking crises.

Default-you never opens the desk, so trading skill and trading mistakes show up in
your grade.

## What you see

Birth starts with the lottery itself: a dot map of the world (Equal Earth projection)
with a circle for each country sized by its share of that year's births, hopping
between likely countries before it lands on yours, and a certificate with your odds.
While you live, your net worth sits on a coin whose metal shows how many years of a
typical local wage you own (copper under one, silver, gold, platinum from ten), its
digits roll as it changes, and a strip shows every year of your life coloured from debt
to wealth, with marks for your milestones and the world's headlines; pick a year to
jump to it in the ledger. Each New Year's Eve a front page sums up the year just gone:
its biggest story, what the stock market did month by month, and your own news. The
statement ends with your best coin and the whole strip.

Under your net worth, a short list shows what moved it last year, biggest causes first:
what you kept from income, interest, each investment's return, your home's value, and
decisions by name (a wedding, a hospital bill), then what rising prices took. The parts
add up to the change exactly; anything not filed under a cause shows as "Other". Next to
your income, your pay is ranked in your country and the world, from the same income
distributions that place your family at birth.

Money moves between generations too. When a parent can't live alone any more you pay
for a care home (their savings first, then the state's share where there is one, then
you and your siblings), move them in with you (a carer by day costs a quarter as much,
and you work a little less), look after them yourself for less pay, or leave it to your
siblings, who then get more of the estate. As they decline the arrangement comes up again:
a live-in carer costs 60% of a care home, fees rise, and siblings can ask you to take
your turn or send money (which wins back part of your share). Grown children may ask for
help with a first home. The statement adds up what you inherited, paid for care, gave and left, and what
each child gets in years of a typical wage. It also draws your life as a picture to
save or share: the coin, your peak wealth, the grade, the strip and the life's code.

A death isn't over in a line. The screen says how and where the life ended, with its last
year and how many of the people born in the same place and year lived longer (from the
same life tables that decide when you die). Then the life passes in a dozen moments
(birth, school, first job, weddings, children, a home, crashes, retirement), one after
another, or as a list if your device asks for less motion, and then the funeral: who came,
what you left, and a gravestone with your dates, the days you lived and an epitaph read from
the life. Building a business, holding on through every crash or making a home far from
where you were born each earn their own line; otherwise it's what you said at 30 money was
for, and whether the life delivered it ("Kept everyone safe." or "Tried to keep everyone
safe."). The stone is a picture to save or share beside the statement card. The statement
then opens with the whole life in a paragraph and its best and worst years for money, after
inflation, each with its biggest cause and the ledger's own line about it.

The life saves itself in your browser as you play. Reload or come back later and
Continue picks up the same year; only one unfinished life is kept, so starting another
asks first.

Names follow the traditions of the 40 countries where most babies are born, including
the main communities inside a country (Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo in Nigeria; Hindu and
Muslim names in India), feminine forms of Russian and Polish family names, and
Myanmar's names without a family name. Partners and children usually share your
family's tradition.

## How a life is scored

When you die, the game replays your life three ways with the same seed (same birth,
same health draws, same markets):

- **Default you** makes the most common choice at every turn.
- **Index-fund you** makes those choices but saves into a broad index and never panics.
- **250 other people** born the same year — anywhere in the world, and in your country
  (plus 60 born in the country you moved to, if you emigrated).

Every life has a code that replays the same birth in the same world: a birth year and a
seed (`1985-1408823311`), or year, country, family and seed for a trip back in time
(`2013-USA-rich-1408823311`). Codes from before birth years were drawn this way are bare
seeds, and still replay.

Average real net worth across adult life is split into three multipliers: where you were
born (your country's median vs. the world's), family and fortune (default-you vs. your
country's median) and your choices (you vs. default-you). The grade S–D measures only
the last one. Time travellers also see what perfect hindsight would have made from the
same savings.

The statement also ranks you among those people at every age, counting only the ones still
alive: in the world, in your country and, from the year you arrived, in the country you moved
to. One chart follows those ranks year by year; the other sets your net worth against the
typical person your age and the middle half and middle 80% of them, in whichever place you
pick, on an axis that shows debts and fortunes together. Above the charts sit three or four
numbers chosen for the life: always your best world rank and roughly how many people born
that year were still ahead of you, then whichever say the most of your rank at home or in
your new country, how you compared with the typical 60-year-old, years in the world's top 10%
or 1%, years in debt, the biggest fall or climb, and where you ended up. Best ranks, falls and
climbs are read from 30, because before then most people own next to nothing and small sums
swing a rank. "Typical" is the median: in a sample this size a single fortune can multiply the
average. Past the richest person sampled, a rank comes from a Pareto tail fitted to the top
tenth, so a large fortune still gets a rank.

## Run it

```
npm install
npm run dev        # play at http://localhost:5173
npm test           # engine tests (determinism, calibration, money rules)
npm run sim        # simulate 300 lives and print wealth and lifespan distributions
npm run build      # static site in dist/
```

Rebuilding the data needs Python 3 with `pandas`, `xlrd` and `babel`:

```
npm run data       # downloads raw sources to pipeline/raw/, writes public/data/world.json
```

The US jobs-by-industry series come through FRED's API, which needs a free key: set
`FRED_API_KEY` first, or save each series' CSV from its FRED page into `pipeline/raw/` as
`fred_<name>.csv` (`fetch.py` prints the names it is missing).

`src/engine/history.ts` adds what the datasets don't carry: headline events, deposit
freezes (Brazil 1990, Argentina 2001, Cyprus 2013, Lebanon 2019…), wars before 1989,
capital controls, university closures, pyramid-scheme waves, and crypto's own record:
exchange failures and repayments, national bans and the big price headlines. The Shanghai Composite's
1990s closes are filled in by hand because the OECD series starts in 2000.

What is simulated rather than replayed: markets in countries with no stock-market data,
gaps in the data, and every year after 2025, drawn from each country's own history.
Over the 30 years after the data, growth, inflation and stock-market swings settle
towards those of a mature economy, so a young market's wildest decades don't repeat
for a century. On the desk, simulated months are drawn so they compound exactly to the year's return
the life engine uses; real months replay the monthly averages in the data. Companies
are fictional; their returns are drawn so that most trail the index and a few carry it.

Exchange rates are the World Bank's. Rates from before a country joined the euro are
converted at the fixed conversion rates, so they line up with national accounts kept in
euros. Where a year's rate implies an impossible price level next to the US (dual
exchange rates, hyperinflation, a currency swapped for the dollar), it is rebuilt from
prices instead.

Where a currency was redenominated so often that old amounts read as zero (Brazil before
1993, the Soviet ruble), money is shown in today's US dollars, marked with ≈. Before a
country's income records begin, wages follow its real income per person, so values in
today's money stay comparable across countries. Taiwan, Venezuela, North Korea, Cuba, Yemen, South Sudan
and Eritrea are missing because the World Bank has no usable economic series for them.

## Layout

```
pipeline/   fetch.py, build.py — raw downloads → public/data/world.json and markets.json
            (monthly history, loaded the first time you open the desk)
src/engine/ the simulation (no UI): economy paths, mortality, careers, businesses,
            events, the yearly loop, automatic players, the end-of-life report and
            the life told at its end (ending.ts);
            markets.ts (monthly prices, companies) and desk.ts (the trading account)
src/ui/     React screens: start, birth lottery and certificate, ledger, decisions,
            trading desk, farewell, statement; the coin, life strip, front page and
            the shareable cards
scripts/    simulate.ts and trace.ts for balancing, geo.mjs for the map,
            artifact-page.mjs for publishing
tests/      vitest suite
```
